Synopsis

Designing Well-being at Scale: My Role in Elevating Thrive Global’s Core Experience

As the sole product designer focused on design systems at Thrive Global, I partnered with 7 cross-functional teams to help shape an experience rooted in mindfulness, science, and behavior change. From reducing stress to building healthier habits, the Thrive app delivers key features that empower users every day:

  • Microsteps – Tiny, science-backed actions that help users make meaningful behavior changes.
  • Thrive Reset – 60-second guided breaks with calming visuals and audio to manage stress in real time.
  • Content Library – Curated articles, videos, and tips that support well-being and productivity.
  • Workplace Integration – Scalable tools that help managers and teams build healthier work cultures.

While collaborating with two senior engineers, I also led the overhaul of the integrated design system used across Thrive’s core products. The system faced real challenges: inconsistent design patterns, outdated components, accessibility and localization gaps, and an overall lack of documentation that led to fragmented implementation.

Although I can’t share most visuals due to privacy, I’ll walk you through the strategic approach I used to bring clarity, consistency, and scalability to the system—laying the foundation for a more seamless product experience.

Too many Lego pieces

Think about this. For the most part, every Lego piece connects to every other Lego piece. There is no silo of green pieces that refuse to connect with the blues. Yellow pieces aren’t busy trying to establish their own kingdom. Larger Lego pieces don’t take over meetings or require more attention. All Lego pieces work together toward a common and unified goal. While that seems like a good idea; being able to interchange and combine any Lego pieces, the output would not be ideal. It can be mismatched and oftentimes, it could be a messy process.

Yes, I’m being a bit lighthearted, but the point is useful. Here are the problems that were happening in our kingdom: low usage in reusable components, unclear guidelines within the documentation, and majority of the components did not meet accessibility and localization standards.

These are some of the valid questions that were asked before we started building the right Lego set for Thrive:

  • How much time and energy is spent when not using the design system (budgets, personnel, and other resources) as opposed to involving one for the unified goal?
  • Does our system encourage connectivity and interdependence?
  • How do we pivot when the current state of the design system no longer serves its purpose?

Test

Wireframes: Low-Fidelity

I began the process of wireframing with sketches of several of the main screens for the Artist Moments social feature. During this process, I thought about how the layout and content could be structured to satisfy user and business goals in a technically feasible way. The sketch I chose served as a guide for my digital wireframes.

Wireframes: High-Fidelity

I created a low-fidelity prototype of the social features in Sketch. I created a UI requirements document to outline all of the features and elements I wanted to incorporate into the design.

During the research phase, I sought to understand the trends that set the standard for the music streaming industry. I also needed to uncover the needs and frustrations of the target user to better understand how to appeal to them.

Usability Testing

  1. Head to John Legend’s Artist Moment, and leave a comment on the video.
  2. View Lady Gaga’s activity in the social feed, and share her music selection to your Instagram Story.
  3. In the Social Feed, change the privacy settings of your Britney Spears post to private.

Findings:

All participants were able to complete all tasks; however there. Navigating to the Artist Moment video and leaving a comment proved to be the most confusing task for users. That task revealed an assumption that I did not realize I had been making: that people would understand the Artist Moments feature based on using similar video features on Instagram and Snapchat.

Results

Even though it was a successful launch and my time was short for the project, I saw significant positive numbers in the data tracking. Here are some takeaways based on the 3 months analysis using a heatmap and the company’s internal tracking tool:

  • 15% increase on Average Time on Page
    •
    • Did A/B testing that included one of the core features. This gave us an indication that users are spending more time on the page due to the relevant data that is structured around the new design system.
  • 45% increase in positive feedback
    •
    • Since it was built for internal teams only, I’ve pushed out a 5-star survey w/ feedback option. We’ve received an overwhelming amount of 4-5 stars feedback.
  • 35% decrease in development hours
    •
    • With the new design system, there's no need in creating new components when there are reusable components.

Reflection

By casting strong villains, it drove urgency and necessity to the design system. Regardless of the project, research is always important. It helps lay a foundation and how we could move forward.

After completing the new design system for all departments, our team felt it was quite rewarding knowing this will push many positive impacts for the company. Even if it is as simple as a static website or a landing page, it will do wonders. If time permits, I would continue to take design ownership and partner with the engineers to ensure the new design system is efficient, secured, and consistent throughout.
